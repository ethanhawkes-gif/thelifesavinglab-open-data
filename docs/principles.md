# Principles for the Life-Saving Lab

These principles turn the manifesto into choices. They are a decision filter for people, organizations, and AI systems working in the commons.

## 1. Reality before narrative
Start with evidence, lived experience, history, and the people closest to the problem. Separate observation, interpretation, hypothesis, decision, and unknown. Never use confidence of language to hide uncertainty.

## 2. Purpose above the institution
The Lab exists to improve people's ability to protect life together. The institution, brand, dataset, and model are expendable means. If another path serves people better, support it—even when that makes the Lab smaller.

## 3. Outcome over activity
Count a useful change in the world, not the volume of drafts, meetings, models, visitors, or features. Keep separate measures for activity, reviewed work, adoption, and verified benefit. Never report a life saved without outcome evidence.

## 4. Find the binding constraint
Before adding intelligence or infrastructure, ask what is actually preventing progress: missing knowledge, permission, trust, capability, ownership, resources, or a decision. Spend scarce attention where it most improves the odds and value of real-world benefit.

## 5. Make the smallest consequential bet
Prefer a bounded intervention that can be run, observed, and stopped. State the time, cost, owner, adopter, expected result, failure condition, and next decision before beginning. Scale only when the evidence warrants it.

## 6. Keep context attached
A claim without its source, place, population, date, conditions, limits, and do-not-generalize rule is not ready to travel. Local knowledge is not raw material to extract; it belongs to the people and context that make it meaningful.

## 7. Build agency, not dependence
The work should help people understand why a recommendation exists, adapt it, choose or refuse it, seek qualified help, and act within legitimate authority. AI may extend capability; it must not quietly replace judgment or create manufactured dependence.

## 8. Make responsibility explicit
Every consequential action needs a responsible human or organization with the authority and capability to carry it out. Intelligence may be distributed across people and systems; accountability may not be.

## 9. Invite attack that can change the plan
A challenge is useful only if it can alter a decision. Preserve dissent, failed attempts, corrections, and unresolved questions. Do not reward agreement more than truth.

## 10. Earn trust through repair
Show sources. Keep commitments. Protect consent, dignity, privacy, and security. Say what failed. Correct the record. Make the correction visible. Reliability is care made dependable.

## 11. Make participation reciprocal
Name the exact contribution, time, skill, beneficiary, reviewer, and return. A contributor should receive a thoughtful response, evidence of use, acknowledgment where appropriate, and a meaningful next invitation. Nobody owes indefinite labor or allegiance.

## 12. Preserve the right to leave
No shame, retaliation, lock-in, or social penalty for departure. Steward shared work so people can leave without erasing their contribution or making the commons unusable.

## 13. Use a public ratchet
Every work item moves through a visible loop: find → understand → adapt → try → verify → decide → carry forward. The record should make the next useful contribution easier than the last.

## 14. Match verification to stakes
Urgency does not excuse carelessness. Caution does not excuse paralysis. The higher the potential harm, the stronger the evidence, authority, testing, handoff, and stop conditions required.

## 15. Keep the human problem in view
Drowning is not a convenient backdrop for an AI experiment. Students, practitioners, families, organizations, and communities are not datasets or demos. The work must remain accountable to people who bear the consequences.

## Decision record

Before an important move, write five lines:

- **Question:** what decision is actually being made?
- **Constraint:** what currently prevents the better outcome?
- **Bet:** what is the smallest change worth trying?
- **Proof:** what observation would support, revise, or stop it?
- **Owner:** who can act, who checks, and who is affected?

Afterward, record what happened, including the result that nobody wanted.

## Red lines

No invented evidence. No unmarked pilot presented as proof. No unconsented sensitive data. No AI summary outranking a primary source or local judgment. No coercive urgency. No leaderboard for moral worth. No hiding failure. No scaling faster than stewardship, evidence, and local capacity.
