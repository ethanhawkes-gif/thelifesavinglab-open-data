History · stations, crews and the log

A building is not a crew.

The American stations, a closer reading of Wood Island, and a few people whose nights still change the tree. Where the sources disagree, both stay. None of this dispatches a boat.

Four beats

How the United States got a service.

The hut, the unmanned shed, the paid crew, the merger. The institution that let a crew go out twice was built in that order.

By 1806

Huts, not a service

The National Park Service traces the American practice to the Massachusetts shore: huts stocked so that whoever saw a wreck could try. By 1806 there were twenty of them. A hut is a cache. It is not a watch, and it is not a crew. The tree still starts at the same question: does someone who can start a rescue know?

National Park Service, “US Life-Saving Service”

1848

Ten thousand dollars and no one on duty

On 14 August 1848 Congress passed the Newell Act, named for William A. Newell of New Jersey, and appropriated $10,000 for surfboats, rockets and stations on that coast. The stations were sheds for gear. Gear without a paid crew is how a system looks like it exists. Sumner Increase Kimball, a Maine-born lawyer, took the Revenue Marine in 1871, inspected the lots, and got authority to employ surfmen.

U.S. Life-Saving Service Heritage Association; the Newell Act of 1848

1878

One superintendent, three kinds of building

In 1878 the Life-Saving Service became its own agency in the Treasury, and Kimball its only General Superintendent. The Park Service distinguishes three buildings: life-saving stations with crews, lifeboat stations, and houses of refuge. A house of refuge was a shelter; no one came from it. By the end, the network was on the order of 280 stations. The number is an agency memory, not a door-count to add to a death toll.

National Park Service; National Coast Guard Museum, on the 1915 reorganization

1915

The merger that ended the name

On 28 January 1915 President Wilson signed the act that joined the Life-Saving Service to the Revenue Cutter Service and made the Coast Guard. Kimball retired with the law that created it. The surfmen did not stop; the letterhead did. Wood Island, built in 1908, was a Life-Saving station for seven years and a Coast Guard station for decades after. The work was the same. The log had to survive the new name.

1827–1908

A quarantine island, then a station

Maine ceded Wood Island to the federal government in 1827. The station association’s history says a hospital stood by 1889, and that Spanish-American War prisoners were held there against yellow fever. The life-saving station is later than the island’s federal life; the story does not start at the boats.

Wood Island Life Saving Station Association

1908

A Lakes plan on a river mouth

Sugden Brothers of Portsmouth built a Duluth-type station to George R. Tolman’s design, for the Life-Saving Service. It replaced Jerry’s Point, station 12, across the harbor in New Castle, which the Navy had taken. Duluth stations were drawn for the Great Lakes. This site is not a sand beach; it is the mouth of the Piscataqua, rocky and fast, which is why the launch is a marine railway and not only a crew dragging a boat through surf. Maine Preservation records it as one of twelve Duluth stations still standing, and the only one with that railway still in place. The apparatus has to match the water: a plan that ignores the railway drills the wrong rescue.

Wood Island Life Saving Station Association; Maine Preservation, 2026

1908–1948

Three counts, not a total

The EPA success sheet says the surfmen saved hundreds of lives in nearly seventy rescues. Maine Preservation, in 2026, says more than sixty major rescues and hundreds of lives across four decades. Down East, in 2022, reported a figure the station’s nonprofit took from its own records: 1,678 seafarers aided between 1918 and 1939. Aided is not saved. Nearly seventy is not more than sixty until someone shows the list, and 1,678 is not the full forty years. The ledger’s rule applies to the building itself: the three are not averaged into a plaque. The row.

U.S. EPA brownfields sheet; Maine Preservation, 2026; Down East, May 2022

1941–1948

The station became a lookout

Early in the Second World War the Navy took Wood Island to watch the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard and to handle anti-submarine nets across the harbor. After the war it reverted to the Coast Guard. The station was decommissioned in 1948. A life-saving station can be turned into a different instrument, and then into nothing, without the wrecks stopping. The living watch for this harbor is Station Portsmouth Harbor, in New Castle. Wood Island does not dispatch.

Wood Island Life Saving Station Association; Maine Preservation, 2026

1948–2016

Demolition lost on the cost of the poison

The association says the island was surplus by 1955. In 1973 the town of Kittery took it, through the federal legacy-of-parks transfer. By 2008 a town committee’s best idea was to tear the station down. Maine Preservation’s account is that abating the hazards cost too much, so the building survived its own demolition vote. In 2011 the new Wood Island Life Saving Station Association was the only proposal. The restoration agreements were signed in 2016. It became a station again only when people took the watch of the building, which is a different watch from the water.

Maine Preservation honor award, 2026; Wood Island Life Saving Station Association

2016–

The money moved, and so did the boat

Down East, in May 2022, had $4.8 million raised. Maine Preservation, in March 2026, had $7.2 million, and named Samuel Reid as the person who led the association to it. Quote the date with the dollar. The same 2026 account describes a rebuilt 1930s eight-oared boat, the Mervin Roberts, put back on the railway. Down East had called a 25.5-foot boat one of about five left, and the only seaworthy one. The classroom in June uses that building. It does not put students in the river. If the drill needs a boat that answers, the boat is in New Castle.

Down East, May 2022; Maine Preservation, March 2026

Six people

What the nights still teach.

Not a pantheon. Each person is here because a branch of the tree is clearer for them. The Pea Island night is now a question in the tree: if you are already on the beach and the boat will not launch, you are no longer in a transit problem.

1871–1915 · Maine, then the Treasury

Sumner Increase Kimball

Kimball did not row the famous boats. From 1871 he inspected a neglected system and put surfmen on wages, with drills. From 1878 he was the only General Superintendent, until the Coast Guard act retired him in 1915. The useful inheritance is not his name on a cutter: a station that does not drill will invent the method on the night, which is how people die in the rescue itself.

National Park Service

1826–1902 · Hull, Massachusetts

Joshua James

Born in Hull on 22 November 1826. The Coast Guard’s history of the cutter named for him says he went off in a Massachusetts Humane Society boat on 17 December 1841, at fifteen, to a wreck at Harding’s Ledge. He became keeper at Point Allerton in 1889. That same history says he and his crew saved 540 lives in the thirteen years he held the station, and that a fire at the Humane Society destroyed many of the earlier records. He died on duty on 19 March 1902, at seventy-five. 540 is their figure for those years, not a lifetime census. A volunteer society can be the real service, and its early book can burn.

U.S. Coast Guard, history of USCGC James

1880–1896 · Outer Banks, North Carolina

Richard Etheridge and the Pea Island crew

Etheridge, born enslaved on Roanoke Island in 1842, a veteran of the 36th U.S. Colored Troops, was appointed keeper at Pea Island in 1880. The Park Service records that the white crew resigned rather than serve under him, and that the station became the service’s only all-Black crew. On 11 October 1896 the schooner E.S. Newman came ashore in a hurricane about two miles south of the station. They could not launch and could not fire the Lyle gun. Two surfmen went off on a line the others held, and the crew brought all nine ashore, including the captain’s wife and their three-year-old son. The Coast Guard names the seven: Etheridge, Benjamin Bowser, Dorman Pugh, Theodore Meekins, Lewis Wescott, Stanley Wise and William Irving. The accounts disagree on the port she sailed from; they agree on the night and the nine. The Gold Lifesaving Medal was awarded on 5 March 1996. He died at the station in 1900. When the boat cannot launch and the gun cannot fire, the method is a tended line, not an untended swimmer.

National Park Service; U.S. Coast Guard Historian’s Office

Keeper, and the medal of 1881 · Lime Rock, Newport

Ida Lewis

On 16 July 1881 Lewis received the Gold Lifesaving Medal, the first woman to hold it. The citation, as usually given, commends her for “rescuing from drowning at various times at least thirteen persons,” and particularly for two soldiers who went through the ice near Lime Rock on 4 February 1881. She kept no records. Later accounts say eighteen lives in fifty-four years on the rock, and unofficial talk has gone to twenty-five. The lighthouse service renamed Lime Rock for her, the only keeper so honored; the Coast Guard named a buoy tender for her. None of that closes the count: the citation says at least thirteen, later tellings say eighteen or twenty-five, and the gap stays.

Gold Lifesaving Medal citation, 1881, as carried in the standard accounts of her life

1894–1947 · Cromer, Norfolk

Henry Blogg

The RNLI’s own account: he joined the Cromer crew in 1894, at eighteen, was coxswain from 1909 to 1947, and in fifty-three years the boat launched 387 times and saved 873 lives. Three Gold Medals, four Silver, the George Cross, the British Empire Medal. In January 1917 the Louisa Heartwell launched four times in fourteen hours, first to the Greek steamer Pyrin, then to the Swedish Fernabo. The crew’s average age was over fifty. The point for this lab is not the decoration: a station can do the night again because the last night was a procedure, written down, by a crew that had launched before. Cromer’s own later note says no one in the institution has been decorated so often. That is their claim, left as theirs.

RNLI, “Henry Blogg: a lifesaving legend,” updated April 2025

1824 · Isle of Man, and a national institution

William Hillary

In 1824, after Hillary’s appeal, the Royal National Institution for the Preservation of Life from Shipwreck was founded. It became the RNLI. He served from the Isle of Man as well as writing the appeal. The number to use is not a personal tally but the institution’s own series: 147,096 lives saved since 1824 in the 2025 accounts, under a definition they print, someone who would have died had the crew not been there. A founder who is only a statue has not saved the next person. A station with a boat, a crew and a log might. The row.
